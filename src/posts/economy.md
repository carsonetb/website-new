There are numerous economic and social theories, all of which seem to have some major downside. For example, with capitalism the downside is exploitation of the working class, and with communism the downside is the necessity of a planned economy and a central authority, which is logistically very hard. With left-libertarianism (and libertarianism in general), problems arise because of the lack of a government to defend and correct the state's economy.

Knowing the downsides of these models, we should try to develop something which prevents major issues such as the ones mentioned before from occuring. First, let's look at all the options, and at some systems which are working well today.

## Liberalism

Western European states are the gold standard of Liberalism, and their quality of life (specifically their health) seems to be better than Americans in most metrics[^1]. This is because, generally, liberal policy is slightly better suited to the working class than the late stage Capitalism in America.

But Liberalism is better than right-wing Capitalism in the same way that eating a spoonfull of salt feels better than a eating a spoonfull of hot sauce---they both don't taste very good. Instead of abolishing the Capitalist upper-class which exploits the lower-class by design, it simply aims to emplace laws which protect the lower-class from the worst of the exploitation (for example by providing free healthcare, public services, and other protections for workers).

Liberalism is a great stepping stone, but if we seek to create a society free of exploitation, we need to look further.

## Authoritarianism

First we must exclude all right-wing ideologies. Right-wing, in this case, has a very specific meaning, although it commonly comes with other baggage. It is the belief that the Capitalist class (those who rent labor for their own profit) should have the most power. By this stricter definition, we see that people whose interests don't align with the core beliefs of Capitalism still identify as right-wing and support right-wing policy. Because of this interest in profit, Capitalism is designed specifically to create a divide in wealth between those who rent their labor and those who buy it. Any person who believes the best society is economically more equal does not align with the economical aspects of the right-wing.

So the goal of our society will instead be to increase equality[^2], especially for the working class. This is the left-wing ideal.

Communism argues that in order to increase equality, there must be a powerful state which manages the economy, ensuring that goods can be distributed to all people. Unfortunately, this method has severe downsides, both of which are responsible for a good part of the western fear of left-wing authoritarianism.

First is the problem of knowledge. In order for a state to be able to manage an economy, it first needs to know what every citizen needs. The only way to effectively do this is to heavily surveil every person in the state. If a state ever does this, any hope of it eventually "[withering away](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Withering_away_of_the_state)" goes away quite fast.

Second is the problem of estimation[^3]. To produce the correct amount of goods to ensure that every person is accommodated requires highly complex calculations and large amounts of data. If these are done wrong, there will either be a surplus or a shortage of goods. In the best cases this is simply a small inefficiency, in the worst it can cause millions of deaths.

Clearly, this style of government is top-heavy, and could not last long before toppling or becoming an undemocratic regime, or both. The natural opposition to this is to build a society which is more spread out, and has no central authority.

## Libertarianism

The left-libertarian ideology is less prominent in history, and has quite a few variations. Fundamentally, libertarianism rejects the idea of the state, but beyond that there are many different models. Some people argue for a more individualist approach (for example allowing the concept of private property). [Others](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murray_Bookchin) argue that groups of people should be organized into municipalities which are self-governing.

Regardless of the specific interpretation, libertarianism faces a fatal flaw. In a world where products cannot be produced everywhere (that is, every society which has advanced beyond the most basic agricultural systems of production), there will be a necessity for a complex supply chain. Unfortunately, if these municipalities tried to enact these supply chains, they would likely not be successful due to an inability to coordinate.

To continue the analogy, this style of "government" is bottom-heavy. We need some sort of overarching government, but we need to be very careful as to how we implement it.

## Middle Ground

The idea that the state can route goods and manage supply chains seems very promising, so we should try to apply it to municipalist model. When the central government was managing the needs of every person (a person is the fundamental unit of the model), we saw that there were problems such as surveilance and complexity.

Instead, the municipalities are the fundamental units. A municipality both imports and exports goods. At the state level, an algorithm takes the requirements of all of the municipalities and the exports of all the municipalities, and routes goods accordingly. If there is a shortage of a certain resource, the algorithm requests that municipalities which have the ability to produce that resource increase their production.

This system seems capable, but naturally an unhandled yet common case arises. What if none of the municipalities want to increase their production? It is much easier to simply continue as normal than to either innovate to make production easier, or to work longer and harder.

To provide an incentive, we make a rule for the algorithm. The value of the resources which a municipality requests must be roughly equal to the value that it exports. In other words, _to each according to its production_. To do this, we need to create some sort of system of value.

In western society, currency is the most common indicator of value. This makes sense in a Capitalist system, but currency really has no place in our system. Instead, we can simply value any good by the average hours it takes to produce across our state[^4]. This way, municipalities that underproduce will be incentivised to optimize or produce a good which is more suitable to its environment. Furthermore, municipalities who are more optimized than the average will be rewarded for their innovation.

But, what if a certain municipality is very inefficient at producing something compared to the average? We don't want to starve this municipality from the outside. Therefore we will modify the valuation system slightly. Instead the algorithm will, based on some ratio decided by the state, choose a value between the average hours and actual hours. This way there is some incentive for innovation (decreasing your actual hours below the average), and also protection from complete failure.

This system is great when it comes to optimization. But at a certain point, optimization leads to a drop in quality which is penalized. When a municipality exports low-quality goods that quickly break or are returned by citizens, a penalty on the hour-value of those goods starts to add up. This way, we are able to algorithmically balance innovation and quality.

In the most extreme cases, there should be a way for municipalities to petition parliament for extra resources or even workers to increase production capacity or respond to natural events.

## Scarcity

There are some cases where there is simply not enough production to meet demand. In this case, the algorithm must decide how to split up supply among municipalities. At the highest level, some goods are simply more valuable to have. If a municipality needs some resource to improve its healthcare, the algorithm should value this more than it should value the production of jewelry. These weights are decided democratically by the state.

At a lower level, citizens in a municipality can choose to pool their labor vouchers towards a common goal, or even to individually request resources from other municipalities which they can use to produce their own goods. 

## Services

This model works well for goods, but what about services? These include the police, fire fighters, healthcare, public transportation, roads, housing, energy, water, and the list goes on.

The way we think about services versus products should be different. Products go _to each according to its output_, but services must go _to each according to its needs._ Therefore, a certain percentage of the national supply will be allocated to developing national services. This way, every citizen receives their fundamental human needs (rights which every person should have) regardless of whether or not they work a job.

## Incentive

One of the classical critiques of Communism is the incentive structure. In Capitalism, people are incentivised by the fear of death—if they don't work they won't make money, and if they don't have money they won't be able to buy food, and if they don't have food they will die. This system of incentives is inhumane. No person should work because they fear death, and it is necessary to develop a better incentive structure.

In modern times there seems to be an opinion that humans are fundamentally driven by greed. It is interesting that this opinion exists, because the psychology, behavioral science, and neuroscience all seem to disagree[^5]. Rather—once basic needs are met—humans desire mastery of skills they enjoy which can be used to improve society.

It seems that because humans are subject to cultural shifts, and in a Capitalist world their motive can be shifted to greed because the only other option is death or poverty. Luckily, because an attitude of societal improvement seems to be the default of human nature, it should be much easier to educate this attitude than its counterpart.

Some jobs, however, are simply too stressful or undesirable to rely on this incentive. For this, the algorithm can step in. Jobs which have more positions than workers to fill those positions are subsidized by the algorithm. The number of vouchers received per hour can be increased to represent the difficulty of the job. The converse is true for jobs which have a high demand, but not many positions.

## Social

After so much optimization of the economic system, a gaping hole has emerged, one upon which the livelihood of our citizens depends. We have so far only thought about the relationship between municipalities and the state. That is because in order to prevent overreach of the state (authoritarianism) we must have fundamental separation between the relationships of state and municipality, and municipality and citizen. They must be independent, and entirely separable.

So now we should consider the relationship between municipality and citizen. Fundamental to our society is a culture of goodwill. Everyone should have a social circle with whom they feel comfortable consistently sharing goods and services with no monetary incentive.

Obviously, this culture cannot possibly extend to intermunicipal import, specialized services, or luxury goods. Furthermore, it does not address the issue of importing goods from the statewide supply chain. We need to have a generic system which can allow a citizen's labor hours to be converted into a method of purchasing specialized or luxury goods of an equivalent value. For this we introduce the concept of "vouchers"---a common concept in Socialist thoguht---which are similar but distinct to a currency. All citizens receive vouchers for food (housing, healthcare, and public transit are _implicit_ and don't require vouchers). Vouchers for anything more are distributed based on the hours a citizen spends making a contribution to society. This is in the form of typical labor, educating themselves, producing artwork, doing open source work, community service, etc.

Vouchers differentiate from typical currency in two important ways. First, they expire after one year. Vouchers are impermanent just like the goods they represent. Second, when a voucher is exchanged for an item, the voucher becomes void and is destroyed[^6].

There are many forms of contribution to society which are rewarded in this system that would typically go unrewarded or at least disincentivized in a Capitalist system. For example, most of the arts, open source hardware and software development, education, and elder care are some of the unpaid or lower paid forms of contribution which people commonly undergo. People who are passionate about these crafts are typically crushed under Capitalist systems.

There will, however, still be a small portion of society who refuse to work in any capacity. In this extreme case, healthcare professionals can be sent to help the person in the likely case that they are experiencing issues like depression or purposelessness. If it happens that the person refuses to work for another reason, such as civil disobedience, society can afford to simply ignore them.

Importantly, in this system there is no case where the state needs to know anything about any specific citizen (only the municipality). Thus, citizens are protected from mass government surveillance and authoritarianism.

## Outside Influence

No modern state is self-sustaining. It has to import necessary resources from other Capitalist countries which will only do business in currency. To do this, our state will build a large and diversified [sovereign wealth fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign_wealth_fund). A certain percentage of global production will be exported to other states in exchange for their currency. This currency can then be invested, stockpiled, or used to buy goods from other states.

Importantly, this fund needs to be very diverse (that is, it is invested in many different countries, currencies, and markets), so that the state can internally balance out the loss of failing sectors with the profit from lucrative ones.

## Government

This short part is going to be more about the political systems and less about the fundamental economics.

The first part of a government is to design a constitution. Everything described before and hereafter should be constitutionally protected. Policy beyond that will be passed by a parliament. Each municipality will send one member to the parliament, which can then vote on policy[^7].

## Transition

There is one problem which looms over every post-Capitalist ideology. That is, how does a state transition from a Capitalist state to a Socialist one? This is an incredibly difficult problem to solve because Capitalism systematically opposes any attempt at subversion. Generally the two forms of transition are through revolution or reform. The two options seem to both have upsides and downsides, and it's difficult to decide which one is better.

I might write about how I would address this problem within the Managed Municipalist system in a future blog post, but for now it is outside the scope of this essay.
