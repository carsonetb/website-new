To write any law that applies to the universe, we must be able to show that it derives from only the universal fundamentals[^1]. To create a good moral code, we must consider consciousness to be among those fundamentals. Otherwise, we would not be able to consider aspects of experience (qualia) when creating this code. 

## Common ground

A human can understand that they themselves are conscious. Because all humans share most of their genetics, and all humans report a sense of qualia, this human can then derive that all other humans experience that same sort of consciousness[^2]. 

If we then take it to be true that all humans share a consciousness, we can begin to build out a moral common ground. 

Pain (that is either physical or emotional pain) is generally reported to be a displeasurable or even agonizing experience. Furthermore, evolutionarily it exists to inform the brain that something bad has happened. Therefore, experiencing a high amount of pain should be considered to be morally undesirable. 

## Evaluation

Any action which leads to a morally undesirable condition carries an amount of negative moral weight. So does an action which increases the amount or intensity of morally undesirable conditions. Oppositely, any action which decreases the amount or intensity of morally undesirable conditions carries a positive moral weight[^3].

Based on these rules, any action can be morally evaluated based on how it effects the conditions for others. This method of derivationism is advantageous because it can show the incorrectness of a moral proposition in and of itself without showing counterexamples where the proposition does not apply.

## Problems

This method of moral reasoning does however have a few problems.

### Death

It is hard to determine the undesirability of death because we do not understand the nature of consciousness. Determining what happens after death is about as difficult as determining what will happen after time, or what happened before. If after death the consciousness enters a constant state of happiness would it then be desirable to die?

Here it makes sense to use a more grounded analogy. Say I own a house in which I live. If someone were to make me the offer to play a game where I could either lose my house, upgrade to a nicer house, or receive something in between, I would ask them what the odds are of each outcome? If they responded that the odds could not be known, I would certainly not take the offer.

### Paradoxes

Some paradoxes can arise because suffering is not precisely measurable, the Trolley Problem and variants being the most famous.
